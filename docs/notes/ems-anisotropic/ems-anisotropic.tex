\title{Anisotropic Energetic Mesh Smoothing with a Metric Tensor Field in Norma}
\author{Alejandro Mota}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Energetic mesh smoothing (EMS) in Norma minimizes a hyperelastic energy of the
mesh, with an ideal reference element per tetrahedron set by a scalar size.
This note records the extension of that ideal element to a metric tensor,
which prescribes three principal sizes and their directions, and the change
in the energy that makes the orientation of the elements part of the
objective: the energy is evaluated on the deformation gradient measured in
the metric, and the stress and tangent are pulled back to the reference
configuration. The formulation, its properties, the treatment of a metric
that varies in space, the implementation, the regression tests, and two
examples are described.
\end{abstract}

\section{Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\paragraph{The isotropic smoothing energy.} For each tetrahedron the ideal
reference element $\Omega_{\bs X}$ is a regular tetrahedron of edge length
$h$, the current element $\Omega_{\bs x}$ is the element of the mesh, and
$\bs F = \partial \bs x / \partial \bs X$ is the deformation gradient of the
map between them. The smoothing energy of the mesh is
$\Phi = \sum_e V_{\bs X}^e\, W(\bs F_e)$, with $W$ an isotropic
hyperelastic energy density (the Seth--Hill family in Norma) and
$V^e_{\bs X}$ the volume of the ideal element. The nodal positions are the
unknowns and the ideal elements are fixed during a solve. Because $W$ is
isotropic, it depends on $\bs F$ only through the invariants of
$\bs C = \bs F^T \bs F$, and $W(\bs Q \bs C \bs Q^T) = W(\bs C)$ for every
rotation $\bs Q$: rotating the ideal element changes nothing. This is
harmless for a regular ideal element, which has no orientation, and fatal
for an anisotropic one: an elongated element has zero energy in every
orientation, so the energy cannot ask the elongation to point in a
prescribed direction.

\paragraph{The metric tensor.} An anisotropic target is a symmetric
positive definite metric tensor
\begin{equation}
  \bs M \;=\; \bs R\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{h_1^2},
  \frac{1}{h_2^2}, \frac{1}{h_3^2}\right) \bs R^T,
  \label{eq:metric}
\end{equation}
with principal sizes $h_i$ and a rotation $\bs R = \exp(\widehat{\bs v})$,
given by a rotation vector $\bs v$, whose columns are the principal
directions. The length of a vector $\bs e$ measured in the metric is
$\sqrt{\bs e \cdot \bs M \bs e}$, so an edge of length $h_i$ along the
$i$-th principal direction has unit length in the metric. Any factor
$\bs F_M$ with $\bs F_M^T \bs F_M = \bs M$ maps physical vectors to
metric-space vectors; Norma uses
\begin{equation}
  \bs F_M \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(1/h_1, 1/h_2, 1/h_3)\, \bs R^T,
  \qquad
  \bs F_M^{-1} \;=\; \bs R\, \operatorname{diag}(h_1, h_2, h_3).
  \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
The ideal element is the unit regular tetrahedron $\Omega_{\bs Y}$ mapped by
$\bs F_M^{-1}$, that is, scaled by $h_i$ along the global axes and then
rotated by $\bs R$: $\bs X = \bs F_M^{-1} \bs Y$. Its volume is
$h_1 h_2 h_3\, V_{\mathrm{unit}}$ with $V_{\mathrm{unit}} = 1/(6\sqrt 2)$.

\paragraph{The energy in the metric space.} The current element is also
mapped into the metric space, $\bs y = \bs F_M \bs x$, and the energy is
evaluated on the deformation gradient between the unit ideal element and
the metric image of the current element,
\begin{equation}
  \bs F_U \;=\; \frac{\partial \bs y}{\partial \bs Y}
  \;=\; \bs F_M\, \bs F\, \bs F_M^{-1},
  \qquad
  \Phi \;=\; \sum_e V^e_{\bs X}\, W(\bs F_{U,e}).
  \label{eq:FU}
\end{equation}
An element has zero energy if and only if its metric image is a unit
regular tetrahedron, that is, if it is a unit element for $\bs M$ in the
sense of metric-based meshing. If the current element is the ideal element
rotated by $\bs Q$, then $\bs F = \bs Q$ and
$\bs C_U = \bs F_M^{-T} \bs Q^T \bs M \bs Q \bs F_M^{-1}$, which equals the
identity only when $\bs Q^T \bs M \bs Q = \bs M$: the orientation is
penalized, except for rotations that leave the metric unchanged, as it
should be. With equal sizes $\bs F_M = \bs I / h$, $\bs F_U = \bs F$, and
the isotropic energy is recovered exactly.

\paragraph{Stress and tangent.} With the ideal volume as the weight,
$\Phi_e = V^e_{\bs X} W(\bs F_M \bs F \bs F_M^{-1})$, and the chain rule
gives the first Piola--Kirchhoff stress and the tangent in the reference
configuration from those of the isotropic model evaluated at $\bs F_U$,
\begin{equation}
  \bs P \;=\; \bs F_M^T\, \bs P_U\, \bs F_M^{-T},
  \qquad
  A_{iJkL} \;=\; (F_M)_{pi}\,(F_M^{-1})_{Jq}\, A^U_{pqrs}\,
  (F_M)_{rk}\,(F_M^{-1})_{Ls},
  \label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
where $\bs P_U = \partial W/\partial \bs F_U$ and
$\bs A^U = \partial \bs P_U / \partial \bs F_U$. Nothing else in the
assembly changes. The same result follows from the nodal chain rule: with
$\bs y_a = \bs F_M \bs x_a$, the nodal forces are
$\bs f_{\bs x} = \bs F_M^T \bs f_{\bs y}$ and the nodal stiffness blocks
$\bs K_{\bs x} = \bs F_M^T \bs K_{\bs y} \bs F_M$.

\paragraph{Properties.}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item The energy does not depend on the factor chosen for $\bs M$: any
  other factor is $\bs Q \bs F_M$ with $\bs Q$ a rotation, which enters
  $\bs F_U$ as a superimposed rigid rotation and leaves the isotropic
  $W$ unchanged. The eigenvector signs and the in-plane phase of $\bs R$
  for repeated sizes are therefore irrelevant to the energy.
\item The orientation of the unit tetrahedron $\Omega_{\bs Y}$ is
  irrelevant for the same reason: every rotation of the unit tetrahedron
  mapped by $\bs F_M^{-1}$ is a zero-energy element. The smoother does not
  prefer one packing of anisotropic elements over another; the mesh
  topology decides.
\item $\det \bs F_U = \det \bs F$, so the volumetric part of the energy
  is unchanged by the metric transformation: it compares the element
  volume with $h_1 h_2 h_3 V_{\mathrm{unit}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{A metric that varies in space}
\label{sec:spatial}

The metric is evaluated once per element at the centroid of the element in
the original mesh, as the isotropic size field is, and held fixed during a
solve. The energy is then an exact function of the nodal positions and the
assembled force is its exact gradient, which the line search and the
convergence tests of the solver require. If the metric followed the
current centroid, $\bs F_M$ would depend on the unknowns and the exact
gradient would contain the derivative of $\bs F_M$, which involves the
derivative of the exponential map of the rotation vector; omitting it
would make the force inconsistent with the energy. The metric is therefore
Lagrangian: an element keeps the target it was assigned where it started.
For smoothing, where nodes move a fraction of an element, this is a
second-order effect. For large motions, or when smoothing alternates with
topological operations, the targets are refreshed at phase boundaries
from the field at the current positions, which is the re-baselining of
the adaptivity note (\code{docs/notes/ems-adaptivity}); each phase then
minimizes a fixed functional.

The metric takes the pseudo-time $t$ as an argument, so a target can be
continued from the sizes of the original mesh to the desired ones over the
pseudo-time steps of the quasi-static solve. Because the sizes and the
rotation vector are given separately, a linear path in $\log h_i$ and in
$\bs v$ interpolates the sizes geometrically and stays a rotation, which is
the interpolation the metric literature recommends.

\paragraph{The volume floor.} Smoothing cannot create elements. The
isotropic \code{size field} rule therefore takes the larger of the size
field and the edge length of the regular tetrahedron with the volume of
the original element, so that no element is asked to shrink below what
the mesh topology can accommodate; without it the size field produced
slivers. The rule \code{metric field} applies the same floor to the
metric: when $h_1 h_2 h_3 V_{\mathrm{unit}}$ is smaller than the volume of
the original element, the three sizes are scaled by the same factor until
the volumes match, so the shape and the orientation of the target are
preserved. The rule \code{metric field unrestricted} omits the floor.

\section{Metrics given as tensors and carried by the nodes}
\label{sec:sources}

The sizes and the rotation vector are the natural input when the target
is designed by hand. A metric that comes from an error estimator, from a
previous run, or from another code comes as a tensor field, usually at
the nodes of the mesh. Norma accepts the metric in four forms: the sizes
with an optional rotation vector, the six components of the tensor, as
expressions in $t, x, y, z$; and the same two forms as nodal variables of
the input mesh, read at a chosen time step.

\paragraph{Factoring a tensor.} The energy needs $\bs F_M$ with
$\bs F_M^T \bs F_M = \bs M$; by property 1 of Section~\ref{sec:formulation}
any factor gives the same energy, so a tensor given by its components is
factored by the eigendecomposition $\bs M = \bs V \bs \Lambda \bs V^T$,
$h_i = \lambda_i^{-1/2}$, $\bs R = \bs V$ with the sign of one column
fixed by $\det \bs R = 1$, and no convention on the frame enters the
input. A tensor that is not positive definite is rejected.

\paragraph{Interpolation between nodes.} A metric carried by the nodes
must be interpolated to the element. The element value is the mean over
the four nodes: of the logarithms of the sizes, which interpolates the
sizes geometrically, and of the rotation vectors, which stays a rotation.
This form keeps the sizes exact where the frame turns: two nodes with the
same sizes and different directions give an element with the same sizes
and the mean direction. The alternative, the mean of the logarithms of
the tensors (log-Euclidean interpolation), preserves the determinant but
not the sizes: two tensors with the same sizes and frames turned by
$90^\circ$ average to a rounder tensor. It needs no frame, however, which
is why it remains as the fallback below.

\paragraph{Following the frame.} The rotation vectors can be averaged
only when the frames of adjacent nodes are the same up to a small
rotation. A tensor determines its frame only up to the 24 proper signed
permutations of its eigenvectors (fewer when sizes are repeated), and an
eigensolver picks one of them arbitrarily at every node. The sizes and
the rotation vector are therefore recovered from nodal tensors with a
frame that follows the mesh: the nodes are visited breadth first through
the element adjacency, and at each node the frame closest to the frame of
the node it was reached from is chosen (the largest trace of
$\bs R_{\mathrm{parent}}^T \bs R$ over the equivalents; the first node of
a component takes the frame closest to the global axes). Repeated sizes
leave the eigenvectors of the repeated pair free in their plane; the
in-plane phase is taken from the projection of the parent frame onto that
plane, and an isotropic tensor takes the parent frame. The rotation vector
is then continued past a half turn from the parent's, so a frame that
turns by more than $\pi$ along a path keeps a continuous vector. Where the
frame turns by more than $45^\circ$ between adjacent nodes after this
choice, the turn cannot be absorbed by a change of representation: the
field has a genuine discontinuity or a singular point of its frame. The
turns are counted and reported, and the input
\code{interpolation: log-Euclidean} switches to the interpolation of the
tensor logarithms, which is insensitive to the frame.

\paragraph{Through the adaptivity loop.} A metric carried by the nodes
is Lagrangian by construction: the node keeps its target when it moves.
Under the topological operations of the adaptivity note, a node added by
an edge split takes the mean of the two ends, in the space the source
interpolates in (logarithms of sizes and rotation vectors, or logarithms
of tensors), a collapse drops the removed node, and the compaction of the
mesh compacts the nodal data with it. The adapted mesh is written with the
nodal variables under the names the input gave them, so the input file
applies unchanged to the adapted mesh. The function sources are simply
re-evaluated where the new elements lie. The size operations of the loop
(collapse below $1/\sqrt2$, split above $\sqrt2$ in the metric) measure an
edge with the metric of its midpoint for the function sources and with
the mean of its two ends for the nodal sources.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
\item \code{MetricField} (\code{src/model\_types.jl}): a source and the
  \code{restricted} flag; stored on the model as \code{metric\_field}.
  The sources are \code{PrincipalMetricFunctions} (compiled size and
  rotation-vector expressions in $t, x, y, z$),
  \code{TensorMetricFunctions} (six compiled components),
  \code{NodalPrincipalMetric} (logarithms of the sizes and rotation
  vectors at the nodes, with the names of the variables they came from),
  and \code{NodalTensorMetric} (logarithms of the tensors at the nodes).
\item \code{create\_metric\_field} (\code{src/metric.jl}): reads the
  \code{metric field} block under \code{model}: exactly one of
  \code{sizes} (with optional \code{rotation vector}), \code{tensor},
  \code{nodal sizes} (with optional \code{nodal rotation vector}), or
  \code{nodal tensor}, the last two from the input mesh at
  \code{time index} (default: the last step) with
  \code{interpolation: principal} (default, frames followed by
  \code{principal\_of\_nodal\_tensors}) or \code{log-Euclidean}.
\item \code{principal\_of\_tensor}: the factorization with the frame
  closest to a reference (24 signed permutations for distinct sizes, the
  in-plane phase from the reference for a repeated pair, the reference
  itself for an isotropic tensor).
\item \code{principal\_metric}: the sizes and rotation of a source on an
  element, at the sampling point for the function sources and as the mean
  over the element's nodes for the nodal sources;
  \code{create\_metric\_reference} calls it, checks the sizes, applies the
  floor, and returns the ideal element $\bs F_M^{-1} \bs Y$ together with
  $\bs F_M$ and $\bs F_M^{-1}$.
\item \code{evaluate\_element!} (\code{src/model.jl}): with a metric
  field, the ideal element is built by the function above, the
  constitutive model is evaluated at $\bs F_U$, and the stress and the
  tangent are pulled back with \eqref{eq:pullback} by
  \code{pull\_back\_tangent}. Without a metric field the code path is
  unchanged.
\item Adaptivity (\code{src/adapt.jl}): \code{metric\_node\_data} gives
  a split node the mean of the ends, \code{metric\_with\_node} appends it
  to the source without a copy for the evaluation of the proposal,
  \code{add\_metric\_node!} and \code{compact\_metric!} keep the nodal
  data aligned with the topology, and \code{metric\_nodal\_variables}
  writes it to the adapted mesh.
\item Input validation knows the keys of the block; the reference
  documentation is in \code{docs/src/reference/model.md}. The sizes and
  rotation vectors at the nodes are written by every source as the nodal
  variables \code{size\_1..3} and \code{rotation\_1..3}
  (\code{nodal\_metric\_output}, with frames followed for the tensor
  sources), so the output of a run can be the input mesh of a nodal run,
  and with them the two tensor forms (\code{nodal\_metric\_tensors}): the
  metric tensor $\bs M = \bs R\,\mathrm{diag}(h_i^{-2})\,\bs R^T$ as
  \code{metric\_xx} and its five companions, and the target tensor
  $\bs T = \bs R\,\mathrm{diag}(h_i)\,\bs R^T$, whose eigenvalues are the
  target edge lengths, as \code{target\_xx} and its companions, which is
  the form a tool that reads scaled eigenvector tensors expects.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tests}
\label{sec:tests}

\code{test/smoothing-metric.jl} (test 126) checks:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item the ideal element and the factor: $\bs F_M \bs F_M^{-1} = \bs I$,
  $\bs F_M^T \bs F_M = \bs M$ with and without rotation, unit edges of
  $\bs F_M \bs X$, the ideal volume, agreement with the isotropic size
  field for equal sizes, the floor (volume of the original element
  recovered, size ratios preserved), evaluation at the reference centroid
  and at the supplied time, and the input errors;
\item the orientation sensitivity on one element with sizes
  $(0.25, 1, 1)$ rotated by $0.4$ rad about $z$: the ideal element has
  zero energy, the same shape rotated by a quarter turn about $z$ has
  energy above one while the isotropic energy on $\bs F$ is zero for
  both, a rotation about the axis of transverse isotropy of the metric
  keeps the energy at zero, and $\bs Q \bs F_M$ gives the same energy as
  $\bs F_M$;
\item the assembled force and stiffness on the cube mesh
  (\code{examples/ems/cube}, 7677 elements) under a metric with all six
  expressions varying in space: central finite differences of the energy
  match the force to $10^{-5}$ relative and a finite-difference
  directional derivative of the force matches the stiffness to
  $10^{-5}$ relative;
\item a smoothing run on the cube with a constant metric at
  $45^\circ$ in the $x$-$y$ plane (sizes $0.5H$, $1.5H$, $1.5H$ with $H$
  the mean edge length): the energy decreases, the mean deviation of the
  edge lengths measured in the metric from unity decreases, and no
  element inverts.
\end{enumerate}
\code{test/smoothing-metric-nodal.jl} (test 132) checks:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item the factorization: sizes and rotation recovered from
  $\bs R\,\mathrm{diag}(h_i^{-2})\,\bs R^T$ for distinct sizes, a large
  rotation followed when the reference is near it and replaced by a
  closer equivalent when the reference is the identity, the repeated pair
  with its in-plane phase from the reference, the isotropic case, twenty
  random tensors and references reproduced by proper rotations, and the
  rejection of an indefinite tensor;
\item the frames followed along a chain of twelve nodes whose frame turns
  by $0.3$ rad per node, past a half turn in total, with continuous
  rotation vectors and no reported turn; a generic turn beyond
  $45^\circ$ from every equivalent, found by search, reported for the two
  adjacent pairs it creates, with every tensor still reproduced;
\item the equivalence of the sources on the cube: the tensor given by its
  components, constant nodal sizes and rotation vectors, constant nodal
  tensors under both interpolations, all give the energy and force of the
  sizes and rotation vector to $10^{-10}$ and $10^{-8}$ relative; nodal
  sizes without a rotation give the sizes along the axes; the variables
  written for an adapted mesh reproduce the input; a graded field
  sampled at the nodes agrees with the analytic field to $2\%$ and not to
  $10^{-8}$; a missing variable, a bad time index, and a nonpositive size
  are rejected;
\item one adaptivity loop of two iterations on the cube with graded nodal
  sizes finer than the mesh, splits only: the adapted meshes carry the
  variables under the input names, the carried data matches the final
  mesh node for node, and every carried size lies within the range of the
  input.
\end{enumerate}
The existing smoothing tests (51, 119), the adaptivity tests (127 to
131), and the input-validation sweep of the examples (122) pass
unchanged.

\section{Examples}
\label{sec:examples}

\paragraph{Cube, constant metric at $45^\circ$}
(\code{examples/ems/cube/cube-metric.yaml}). Sizes $0.05$, $0.1414$,
$0.1414$ (the volume of the mesh size $0.1$), first principal direction
at $45^\circ$ in the $x$-$y$ plane, unrestricted rule, nodes fixed in the
normal direction on the faces, L-BFGS. Over the edges of the mesh the mean
of $\lvert\, \lVert \bs F_M \bs e \rVert - 1 \rvert$ goes from $0.44$ to
$0.31$, the mean edge component along the small direction from $0.055$ to
$0.042$ (target $0.05$), and the mean component across it from $0.085$ to
$0.098$. The number of elements is fixed, so the metric cannot be met
everywhere; the elements rotate and stretch toward it.

\paragraph{Tube, graded axial size}
(\code{examples/ems/tube/tube-metric.yaml}). The rotation vector
$(0, -\pi/2, 0)$ carries the first principal direction onto the axis of
the tube; the axial size grows linearly from $0.06$ at the bottom to
$0.18$ at the top while the radial and circumferential sizes stay at the
mesh size $0.12$, restricted rule, nodes sliding on the four analytic
surfaces. The mean axial size equals the mesh size, so the grading can be
realized by sliding without a change of topology. The mean of
$\lvert\, \lVert \bs F_M \bs e \rVert - 1 \rvert$ over the edges goes
from $0.18$ to $0.12$, and the mean length of the axial edges (edges with
more than $70\%$ of their length along $z$) in the four quarters of the
height, from bottom to top, goes from $0.105$, $0.109$, $0.109$, $0.105$
to $0.075$, $0.110$, $0.127$, $0.144$, against targets of $0.075$,
$0.105$, $0.135$, $0.165$ at the quarter centers. The largest nodal motion
is $0.28$, about two element sizes, with no element inversion.

\paragraph{The same metric as a tensor and from the nodes}
(\code{examples/ems/cube/cube-tensor.yaml},
\code{examples/ems/cube/cube-metric-nodal.yaml}). The cube metric above
as the tensor $M_{xx} = M_{yy} = 225$, $M_{xy} = 175$, $M_{zz} = 50$, and
as the nodal variables \code{size\_1..3}, \code{rotation\_1..3} of the
output of \code{cube-metric.yaml} read at its first step. A constant
field has the same mean at the nodes as its value at the centroid, so the
nodal run starts from the energy and residual of the analytic run
($4.32 \times 10^4$ and $8.54 \times 10^4$) and ends at the same energy
($9.36 \times 10^3$). The tensor run ends at $9.37 \times 10^3$: its
component $M_{zz} = 50$ corresponds to the size $0.14142$ rather than
$0.1414$, a difference of $0.015\%$ in the target that the energy
resolves.

\paragraph{What smoothing cannot do.} A target that changes the number
of elements across the wall or around the circumference, such as a
radial size of a quarter of the wall thickness on a mesh with one element
through the wall, is not reachable by smoothing: the mesh barely moves and
the misfit does not decrease. Such targets require the topological
operations of the adaptivity note.

The same obstruction has a sharper form on a closed domain, found while
answering a question from Jay Foulk. On the unit cube meshed at $0.1$, with
the six faces held as planes, an axis-aligned target of sizes
$(0.025, 0.2, 0.2)$ leaves the mesh exactly isotropic: the mean edge
component stays $(0.052, 0.052, 0.052)$, and the solve stops at a strict
local minimum, with the gradient below $10^{-8}$ and the Hessian positive
definite (smallest eigenvalue 399 over 4140 free degrees of freedom). A
randomly displaced initial mesh, whose worst scaled Jacobian is $0.097$
and whose energy starts at $1.10 \times 10^5$ instead of
$8.06 \times 10^4$, converges to the same energy, $7.2675 \times 10^4$:
the configuration is fixed by the constraints, not by the path. The reason
is arithmetic. The faces are fixed planes, so the extent across $x$ is
one, and the number of elements along a path across the mesh is what the
connectivity gives, so the mean advance per element is pinned near $0.05$;
a target of $0.025$ asks for twice as many layers as exist. Milder
axis-aligned targets, $(0.05, 0.1414, 0.1414)$ and $(0.05, 0.2, 0.1)$,
leave the mesh isotropic for the same reason.

The same metric rotated by $45^\circ$ is partly reachable, because the
thin direction is no longer normal to a pair of fixed faces and a path
through the mesh can zigzag in the plane normal to it: the mean edge
component along the thin direction goes from $0.052$ to $0.029$ against
the $0.025$ asked, and the energy falls by a factor of five. Ramping the
sizes from the mesh size to the target over the pseudo-time of the solve
reaches the same minimum as the direct target, so this is not a matter of
following the minimum. The remedy is topology: the same axis-aligned
target under the adaptivity loop takes the energy from
$7.27 \times 10^4$ to $9.4 \times 10^2$ over twelve iterations, at 10663
elements from 7677, and the mean edge components to
$(0.0136, 0.090, 0.092)$ against the $(0.0125, 0.1, 0.1)$ that the target
implies, with $96.8$ percent of the edges inside the band measured in the
metric and every boundary node still on its face. The same target at
$45^\circ$ ends at $(0.0136, 0.093, 0.089)$ and $96.4$ percent: with
topology allowed the two orientations agree, as they must, and the
difference that node motion alone shows between them is an artifact of
what the fixed connectivity permits. The two examples are
\code{examples/ems/cube/cube-metric-adaptive.yaml} and
\code{cube-metric-rotated-adaptive.yaml}, which run the same target in the
two orientations and end at 10801 and 10768 elements with mean edge
components $(0.0134, 0.091, 0.091)$ and $(0.0134, 0.094, 0.090)$ and
$96.8$ and $96.2$ percent of their edges inside the band. Each smoothing
phase ends on the energy stagnation exit after 9 to 18 L-BFGS iterations,
and the twelve alternations take four minutes on eight threads.

\section{Next steps}
\label{sec:next}

The nodal metrics, the tensor input with the frame followed through the
mesh and the log-Euclidean fallback, and the coupling with the
topological operations of the adaptivity note are done
(Section~\ref{sec:sources}). What remains:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
\item Construction of the metric from a solution: the recovered Hessian
  of a field of interest, scaled to a prescribed error or element count,
  written as nodal tensors that the nodal source reads.
\item A frame that follows the mesh cannot be continuous around a
  singular point of the field (a swirl); there the log-Euclidean fallback
  applies to the whole mesh. A local switch, or a frame taken from a
  smoothed rotation field, would keep the exact sizes elsewhere.
\end{enumerate}

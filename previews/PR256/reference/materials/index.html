Materials

Materials are declared inside the model block. The material subsection maps each mesh element block to a named material definition, and each named definition selects a constitutive model and its parameters:

model:
  type: solid mechanics
  material:
    blocks:
      cantilever: steel      # element block "cantilever" uses material "steel"
    steel:                   # the material definition named above
      model: linear elastic
      elastic modulus: 6.895e+09
      Poisson's ratio: 0.25
      density: 2768.0

The names on the left of blocks must match the element block names in the Exodus mesh, and the number of entries must equal the number of element blocks. The name on the right is an arbitrary label that must itself appear as a key under material, holding that material's parameters.

Elastic constants

Every isotropic constitutive model draws its elastic response from exactly two elastic constants (the anisotropic models take the constants of their symmetry class instead; see Anisotropic elasticity). Supply any valid pair from the table below; Norma computes the remaining constants. Supplying zero, one, or an unsupported combination aborts.

KeySymbolConstant
elastic modulusEYoung's modulus
Poisson's ratioνPoisson's ratio
bulk modulusκbulk modulus
Lamé's first constantλfirst Lamé parameter
shear modulusμshear modulus

Accepted pairs: elastic modulus with any one of the other four; Poisson's ratio with bulk modulus, Lamé's first constant, or shear modulus; bulk modulus with Lamé's first constant or shear modulus; or Lamé's first constant with shear modulus. The key spellings are literal, including the apostrophe in Poisson's ratio and the accented, apostrophized Lamé's first constant.

density (ρ) is optional and defaults to 0.0. It is not used by static analyses but is required in practice by the dynamic time integrators, which need a mass matrix.

elastic modulus: 6.895e+09
Poisson's ratio: 0.25
density: 2768.0

Parameters: two elastic constants, optional density.

Seth-Hill

Generalized hyperelastic model with tunable strain-measure exponents m and n (both required integers).

model: seth-hill
elastic modulus: 6.895e+09
Poisson's ratio: 0.25
density: 2768.0
m: 2
n: 2

Parameters: two elastic constants, optional density, required integers m and n.

Hencky

Hyperelasticity quadratic in the logarithmic strain,

\[\psi = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\,(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{E})^2 + \mu\,\operatorname{dev}\mathbf{E} : \operatorname{dev}\mathbf{E}, \qquad \mathbf{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}\log\mathbf{C}.\]

Under uniaxial stress the lateral log stretch is exactly $-\nu$ times the axial one and the Kirchhoff stress is exactly $E \log\lambda$ at any stretch, which makes this model useful for testing strong nonlinearities against closed-form answers.

model: hencky
elastic modulus: 1.0e+09
Poisson's ratio: 0.25
density: 1000.0

Parameters: two elastic constants, optional density.

J2 plasticity

Finite-deformation J2 plasticity with a multiplicative elastic–plastic split, radial-return integration, and linear isotropic hardening.

Anisotropic elasticity

The anisotropic models take their elastic constants in the frame of the material's lattice (for a crystal, the cube axes; for a hexagonal crystal, lattice axis 3 is the c axis), and an orientation that places the lattice in the sample. anisotropic linear elastic is the small-strain law σ = ℂ : ε; anisotropic Saint-Venant Kirchhoff is its finite-strain counterpart, linear between the Green–Lagrange strain and the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress, and the elastic law of LCM's crystal plasticity model.

model: anisotropic Saint-Venant Kirchhoff
symmetry: cubic
C11: 168.4e+09
C12: 121.4e+09
C44: 75.4e+09
density: 8960.0
orientation:
  Bunge Euler angles: [30.0, 45.0, 0.0]   # degrees unless `angle unit: radians`

The constants are the components of the Voigt stiffness, in the order 11, 22, 33, 23, 13, 12 with engineering shear strains, so that C44 relates σ₂₃ to 2ε₂₃. The symmetry key selects which of them are given:

symmetryConstantsNotes
isotropictwo elastic constantsas for the isotropic models
cubicC11, C12, C44
hexagonalC11, C12, C13, C33, C44C₆₆ = (C₁₁ − C₁₂)/2; transversely isotropic is a synonym
tetragonalC11, C12, C13, C33, C44, C66
orthotropicC11, C22, C33, C12, C13, C23, C44, C55, C66
triclinicany of the 21 Cij, i ≤ jthe others are zero

The constants must give a positive definite stiffness; otherwise the input is rejected. For explicit dynamics the stable time step uses the largest wave modulus ρc² over all propagation directions in place of λ + 2μ.

Lattice orientation

The orientation Q holds the lattice axes, expressed in the sample frame, as its columns. A lattice direction v has sample components Q v, and the elasticity tensor in the sample frame is

\[\mathbb{C}_{ijkl} = Q_{ip}\,Q_{jq}\,Q_{kr}\,Q_{ls}\,\mathbb{C}^{L}_{pqrs}.\]

The orientation block takes one of:

KeyValue
basis vectorsthe three lattice axes in the sample frame, [[...], [...], [...]] (LCM's Basis Vector 1/2/3); each is normalized, and the triad must be orthogonal and right-handed
Bunge Euler angles[φ1, Φ, φ2], giving Q = Rz(φ1) Rx(Φ) R_z(φ2)
mesh variablesnames of element variables of the input mesh that give each element its own orientation: three names for Bunge Euler angles, or nine for Q row-major (Q₁₁, Q₁₂, …, Q₃₃)

angle unit (degrees, the default, or radians) applies to the Bunge Euler angles, also when they come from the mesh, and time index (default 1) selects the step of the mesh variables. Without an orientation, the lattice axes coincide with the sample axes.

The basis vectors follow the convention of LCM's crystal plasticity model. LCM versions before commit 42ba4a5 rotated the elasticity tensor by Qᵀ instead (sandialabs/LCM#127); Norma reproduces purely elastic results of those versions with the inverse orientation, the Bunge angles (−φ2, −Φ, −φ1).

Anisotropic hyperelastic damage

A compressible neo-Hookean matrix reinforced by any number of fiber families, each phase degraded by a damage that grows with the largest strain energy it has stored. This is LCM's AHD model:

\[\begin{aligned} W_m &= \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}(J^2 - 1) - \log J\right] + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\left(J^{-2/3}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C} - 3\right), \\ W_f &= \frac{k}{q}\left[e^{q (I_4 - 1)^2} - 1\right], \qquad I_4 = \mathbf{M} \cdot \mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{M}, \\ d &= d_{\max}\left[1 - e^{-\alpha / s}\right], \qquad \alpha = \max_{\text{history}} W, \\ \mathbf{P} &= \phi_m (1 - d_m)\,\frac{\partial W_m}{\partial \mathbf{F}} + \sum_f \phi_f (1 - d_f)\,\frac{\partial W_f}{\partial \mathbf{F}}. \end{aligned}\]

Without damage, the matrix is the neohookean model. As in LCM, the fibers resist compression as well as tension. A fiber stretch at which e^{q (I_4 − 1)^2} would overflow, which a long Newton trial step can reach, fails the evaluation, and the step is cut.

elastic modulus: 2.0e+05          # matrix
Poisson's ratio: 0.3

matrix maximum damage: 0.1
matrix damage saturation: 1.0e+04
fibers:
  - direction: [1.0, 0.0, 0.0]    # in the material frame
    k: 1.0e+04
    q: 100.0
    volume fraction: 0.5
    maximum damage: 1.0
    damage saturation: 1.0e+04
orientation:                      # optional; this one turns the fiber to y
  Bunge Euler angles: [90.0, 0.0, 0.0]
KeyDefaultMeaning

The stable explicit time step uses the stiffness of the undeformed material, φm (λ + 2μ) + Σf 8 φ_f k. The exponential fibers stiffen fast with their stretch: for q = 100 the largest wave modulus is 3 times the estimate at 5 % fiber stretch and over 200 times at 10 %, so an explicit run with stretched fibers needs a CFL that allows for it.

The internal variables are the largest energy and the damage of the matrix and of each fiber family, matrix_energy, fiber_1_energy, …, matrix_damage, fiber_1_damage, …. LCM keeps the energy of the previous step instead of the largest over the history, so its damage recovers on unloading; the two agree under monotonic loading.

Crystal plasticity

Finite-deformation, rate-dependent crystal plasticity, ported from LCM's CrystalPlasticity model. The deformation gradient splits as F = Fe Fp. A slip system α has a unit direction s and normal n in the lattice frame; with the lattice orientation Q its Schmid tensor is Pα = (Q sα) ⊗ (Q n_α), and over a step the plastic part follows the exponential map

\[\mathbf{F}^p_{n+1} = \exp\Big(\sum_\alpha \Delta\gamma_\alpha \mathbf{P}_\alpha\Big)\,\mathbf{F}^p_n .\]

The elastic law is the anisotropic Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff law on Fe, and the resolved shear stress is the Mandel stress on the system, τα = (Feᵀ Fe S) : Pα. Each system belongs to a slip family, whose flow rule gives the slip rate from τα and the slip resistance gα, and whose hardening law gives the hardening state from the slip rates.

symmetry: cubic

The implicit scheme solves for the slips (or, with residual: slip and hardness, for the slips and the hardening states) by Newton's method, with the tolerances of an optional local solver block (absolute tolerance, default 1e-12; relative tolerance, default 1e-14; minimum iterations, default 0; maximum iterations, default 50). The explicit scheme evaluates the resolved shear stress on Fpn and the hardening with the slip rates of the previous step; it is only stable while Δt (∂γ̇/∂τ) μ < 2. The implicit scheme starts its Newton iteration from the slip rates of the previous step (slip predictor: rate, the default) or from zero rates (slip predictor: none). The explicit scheme needs rate: with none its hardening would never change, as in LCM, and the input is rejected. With residual: slip, as with the explicit scheme, the hardening is integrated by forward Euler, as in LCM. For saturation hardening this overshoots the saturation stress when a step slips more than about (gs − g₀)/(2 h₀) in all, and a step that crosses it is a local failure; residual: slip and hardness integrates the hardening implicitly and takes such steps. A local failure cuts the global step under adaptive time stepping, and stops the run without it. Without a predictor, the first Newton iterate of a quasi-static step puts the whole increment of a prescribed displacement on the elements next to the boundary, so the time step must keep that strain within the range of the flow rule; with predictor: tangent the step is no longer bound to the size of those elements. The tangent is the exact algorithmic tangent, which is not symmetric: use linear solver: direct with the Hessian minimizer.

The internal variables are Fp_ij, then gamma_α, gamma_dot_α, tau_hard_α (the hardening state: the slip resistance, or the dislocation density) and tau_α for every system, then eqps.

Polycrystals

A polycrystal is a mesh whose elements carry their own lattice orientation as element variables, read with orientation: {mesh variables: [phi1, Phi, phi2]}; one material then serves every grain. The generators in examples/crystal-plasticity write such meshes: make_rubiks_cube.jl builds LCM's RubiksCube, 27 cubic grains with LCM's orientations, and microstructpy_to_exodus.jl converts a Voronoi polycrystal from MicroStructPy to TETRA4, TETRA10 or HEX8 elements, with an orientation drawn uniformly over SO(3) for every grain (Norma.uniform_random_rotation; Norma.bunge_angles gives its angles).

Three material-point drivers evaluate a constitutive model without a mesh. Each takes the times at which to evaluate, and the components of F listed in free (a 3 × 3 Boolean matrix) are solved for so that the same components of the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress follow stress(t) (zero by default):

FunctionResponse
Norma.material_point_history(material, deformation, times; orientation, free, stress)one material point under F = deformation(t)
Norma.taylor_history(materials, orientations, weights, deformation, times; free, stress)Taylor (iso-strain) aggregate: every grain under the same F, with the free components set on the volume-averaged stress
Norma.sachs_history(materials, orientations, weights, axial_strain, times)Sachs (iso-stress) aggregate in uniaxial stress along x: every grain under the same P = s e₁ ⊗ e₁, with s such that the average of F₁₁ − 1 is axial_strain(t)

The aggregates take one material and one orientation per grain and the grain volumes as weights, and return the averaged F, P and Cauchy stress at every time, with the deformation and state of each grain. In elasticity they are the Voigt and Reuss bounds of a displacement-based finite element solution of the polycrystal: the Taylor response is stiffer, provided the boundary conditions admit its uniform F (with rollers on the faces of a cube, only the diagonal of F may be free), and the Sachs response is softer, as its uniform stress is statically admissible. With plasticity they are estimates, not bounds: the grains of the aggregates and of the finite element solution carry different histories, and the incremental update of a rate-dependent crystal is not variational, so nothing guarantees the order, although a correct solution is expected to lie between them, as it does in the tests and the examples.
